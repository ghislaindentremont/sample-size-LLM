Power vs. sample size

What is being tested

Let p be the true probability that a response from this model, on this task distribution, is judged acceptable. The test asks whether the data are inconsistent with the model performing at or below the threshold:

H₀: p ≤ p₀   versus   H₁: p > p₀

You judge N responses and count the acceptable ones, X. H₀ is rejected when X is at least the critical count shown in the result panel. The expected proportion p₁ is not tested; it is the alternative you plan around, and power is the probability of rejecting H₀ if p is truly p₁.

Assumptions that make this test valid

Each judgment is binary and the rubric is fixed in advance.
Every response is acceptable or not, by criteria decided before any responses are seen. Changing the rubric after looking at outputs makes the observed rate uninterpretable.
Responses are independent.
One response per prompt, from prompts that do not share content. Multiple samples of the same prompt or multi-turn conversations are correlated: they count as fewer than N independent trials and inflate the false-positive rate.
The prompts are a random sample from the distribution you want to make a claim about.
The test estimates one proportion for one population. If your prompts are easier or differently mixed than deployment traffic, p refers to your prompt set, not to the deployment.
N is fixed before data collection.
Decide the sample size here and judge all N responses. Stopping early or adding responses based on interim results raises the true α above the nominal value.
Judgments are accurate.
The test treats each label as truth. A lenient judge inflates the observed rate; measure judge agreement separately.
The question is one-sided.
You want evidence that performance exceeds p₀ and would not act on evidence that it is lower. A non-significant result does not show performance is below p₀; it only fails to show it is above.
Score/CC methods only: N is large enough.
The z-tests need both N·p₀ and N·(1−p₀) to be at least about 10. With thresholds near 1, such as 0.90, the failure count is small and the approximation is poor below roughly N = 100. The exact test has no such requirement.

How the numbers are computed

These are analytic calculations — not Monte Carlo simulations. Every number comes from evaluating a closed-form expression or summing an exact probability distribution. No random experiments are simulated. A Monte Carlo study would generate fake datasets, run the test on each, and count rejections; the analytic approach gives the same answer directly, in milliseconds and without sampling error.

Exact binomial. Under H₀ at the boundary (p = p₀), X ~ Binomial(N, p₀). The critical count c is the smallest integer with P(X ≥ c | p₀) ≤ α. Power is P(X ≥ c | p₁). Both probabilities are computed by summing binomial terms exactly, using a log-space recurrence to avoid underflow at large N. For sample size, N is the smallest value whose power reaches the target — found by scanning N = 1, 2, 3, … Because the binomial is discrete, the achieved α is at most the nominal α, and power does not increase smoothly with N; the sawtooth in the chart shows this.

Score (Wilson) z-test. The score test evaluates the standard error at p₀ (the null proportion) rather than at the sample estimate p̂. This is equivalent to requiring the one-sided Wilson score confidence bound to exceed p₀ — which is why the Wilson interval is better than Wald near 0 and 1, and the same logic applies to the test. Sample size and power use a consistent continuous approximation:

N     = ⌈ ( (z_α·√(p₀q₀) + z_β·√(p₁q₁)) / (p₁ − p₀) )² ⌉
power = Φ( ( (p₁ − p₀)·√N  −  z_α·√(p₀q₀) ) / √(p₁q₁) )
crit  = ⌈ N·p₀ + z_α·√(N·p₀q₀) ⌉

Score z-test + continuity correction. Adds a ½ adjustment to the rejection boundary: c = ⌈N·p₀ + z_α·√(N·p₀q₀) + ½⌉. Power is evaluated at this discrete threshold with a CC on the alternative side. The test is more conservative (closer to the exact result) and its achieved α ≤ nominal α. The two score methods converge toward the exact result as N grows; when they disagree, the exact result is the one to report in a publication.

The Beta-Binomial model

In many LLM evaluation studies, questions differ in inherent difficulty — some prompts are much harder than others. When the LLM gives k responses per question, the responses to the same question are correlated: they share the same underlying difficulty pi. The standard binomial model ignores this correlation and treats all n·k responses as independent. The Beta-Binomial model accounts for it.

The model assumes each question i has its own acceptable-response probability pi, and that these per-question probabilities follow a Beta distribution:

pi ~ Beta(αβ, ββ)    Xi | pi ~ Binomial(k, pi)

The marginal mean is μ = αβ/(αβ+ββ) and the Beta concentration is φ = αβ+ββ. Higher φ means the pi are tightly clustered near μ (less question-to-question variability). The intra-class correlation ICC = ρ = 1/(1+φ) is the proportion of total variance in individual responses that comes from question-level differences. When ρ = 0, all questions are equally hard and the model reduces exactly to the standard binomial.

The design effect and variance inflation

The key quantity is the variance inflation factor (also called the design effect):

VIF = 1 + (k − 1) · ρ

The total observed proportion p̂ = X/(n·k) has variance μ(1−μ)·VIF / (n·k), compared to μ(1−μ)/(n·k) for iid responses. The clustering inflates the variance by VIF. Equivalently, the effective sample size is n·k/VIF — fewer independent pieces of information than the raw count suggests.

When k = 1, VIF = 1 regardless of ρ: a single response per question means there is no within-question replication and no inflation. VIF grows with both k and ρ. For k = 5 and ρ = 0.15, VIF = 1.6, meaning you need 60% more total responses than the standard formula says.

Ignoring clustering inflates your Type I error. If you use the standard binomial test on Beta-Binomial data, the true significance level is 1 − Φ(zα/√VIF), not α. For k = 3 and ρ = 0.10 (VIF = 1.2), a nominal 5% test actually rejects at 6.7%. Your power estimate is also overstated — true power is lower than the formula predicts.

The score test for the Beta-Binomial model

The test is H₀: μ ≤ p₀ vs H₁: μ > p₀, where μ = E[pi]. The score test statistic uses the VIF-adjusted standard error under H₀. The power formula and sample size are identical to the score test on Tab 1, with the total N replaced by the effective sample size N/VIF:

n = ⌈ VIF · (z_α·√(p₀q₀) + z_β·√(μ₁q₁))² / (δ² · k) ⌉  (questions)
N = n · k                                                   (total responses)

power = Φ( ( (μ₁ − p₀)·√(n·k/VIF) − z_α·√(p₀q₀) ) / √(μ₁q₁) )

The critical count of total successes X is c = ⌈n·k·p₀ + z_α·√(n·k·p₀q₀·VIF)⌉. All computations are analytic (CLT-based) — there is no exact closed-form BB distribution for arbitrary n, so no exact test is offered here. The CLT approximation is reliable when n is at least 30 questions and n·p₀ and n·(1−p₀) are both at least 5.

Assumptions

Each question's difficulty pi is fixed and drawn i.i.d. from Beta(αβ,ββ).
The Beta distribution is a modeling assumption. If question difficulty follows a very different distribution, the VIF still characterizes the variance inflation but the CLT convergence may be slower.
k responses per question are generated independently given pi.
The LLM samples each response at the same temperature and context, independently. If responses within a question share a conversation history or cache, they are more correlated than the model assumes, understating the true VIF.
All questions have the same number of responses (equal k).
Unequal k per question requires a weighted analysis. If k varies, use the harmonic mean of k as a conservative approximation.
Questions are a random sample from the target task distribution.
The test estimates the mean rate for one population of tasks. A convenience sample of easy or atypical questions leads to a μ that does not generalize.
N = n·k is fixed before data collection.
Same as the one-proportion test: sequential stopping inflates the true α.
Judgments are accurate and the rubric is fixed.
Same as the one-proportion test: observer bias shifts the observed proportion.
